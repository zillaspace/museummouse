    Pilot candidates

    The plan, below
    Draft plan for review. Mark Johnston, September 2026.

    A museum's collection, made into a film worth watching.

    Museum Mouse is a producer led service. It takes what a museum already owns, the photographs, footage, objects, research and voices, and turns it into a documentary the museum could never afford to commission. Three to five minutes is the standard shape; twenty minutes with a full narrative arc is possible. The same engine can later tell a city's ghost tour or a small business's best moments. Museums come first because every one of them has a story and almost none of them has a film.

    Room02

    Who buys, and how we find them

    Who's going to buy this?

    Start with regional museums and historical societies. They have the story and none of the budget, and the champion inside the building is almost always exhibitions or education. The signature belongs to the director, and the money usually comes from outside: a donor, a foundation, a tourism board, a friends of the museum group.

    What gets us in the door

    • An exhibition opening on a date, which agents can track from museum calendars and press pages.
    • An outdated introductory film. The Vasa Museum's theater film in Stockholm has run for roughly two decades; every country has a hundred of these.
    • Languages: international visitors and no way to serve them.
    • A donor who has wanted a specific story told and now can be offered a price that fits inside one gift.
    • Accessibility deadlines for publicly run institutions.

    Customer qualification, three tests

    Usable assets they can hand over. One curator who can approve facts. A real deadline. Without all three the job drifts; with all three it ships.

    Low cost countries first, or go straight to the Met?

    Both, in sequence. The pilots are three small museums in three different situations, because the proof that matters is "highest quality at the lowest cost museum." Once three films exist, the Met conversation is a screening, not a pitch. Big institutions are the fast track for credibility; small ones are the reason the company exists and the bulk of the 104,000.

    Pricing to place

    The map already knows the country and the museum's size. A pricing table keyed to country income tier and operating band means Iceland is not quoted the New York number. Three bands to start: standard, reduced (about 60 percent), and sponsored (free to the museum, paid by a funder).

    Numbers to test on the pilots, not a rate card. Per film fixed fees, not hourly: the museum wants one number, and hourly billing punishes the whole point, which is that AI collapses the hours. A retainer (about 25 production hours a month) is an option for institutions that want a steady stream of updates.

    Rounds are the lever

    Two rounds included. The price only works if the first cut is close, so the intake and the curator approval of the script carry the weight. Extra rounds are paid, always, including for friends.

    How do I get this so intuitive that I can make the calls fast?

    A dashboard per film with five decisions and nothing else: which treatment, approve script, approve voice, approve cut, ship. Everything the AI did sits behind each decision as evidence, one click away. If a decision takes longer than the film runs, the pipeline is wrong, not the producer.

    Funding read: the pilot phase is under $10,000 in tools for three months. The fifty a week machine is roughly $1.8 million a year all in, against revenue of $7 million to $10 million at the Standard and Plus prices if the museums are there. The pilots tell us whether they are.

    Hardware

    The heavy compute lives in the vendors' clouds, so there is no GPU farm to buy. Each producer needs a strong laptop (an M5 Max class machine, $4,000 to $6,000), a calibrated reference monitor ($1,500), fast local storage (a 40 TB NAS, $2,500, shared by up to five producers), and gigabit internet. Call it $8,000 to $10,000 per seat, once. At fifty films a week, add cloud render and storage of about $2,000 a month.

    Delivery and hosting

    Do not build a Dropbox. Use Frame.io for review and approval (the curator comments on the frame) and Dropbox or Google Drive for delivery through the pilots. When there are fifty clients, a simple client portal on cloud storage (Cloudflare R2 or S3) with a login per museum, still no servers of our own. Masters are archived in cold storage for the refresh business; that archive is an asset.

    The pilots

    Aspen, Sweden, or the three remotest places on Earth?

    One of each. That is three pilots, three to five minutes, free, in exchange for a case study, a testimonial, and their honest data on what a film would have cost them.

    The Aspen Historical Society or the Aspen Art Museum. Fastest access, existing relationships, and a film that can be screened locally within weeks. Proves the process.

    Heritage

    A small museum near Halmstad, where Mark was born, with the Vasa Museum's twenty year old theater film as the dream target in the same trip. Proves language: Swedish and English from one master.

    Remote

    The far corner

    The Húsavík Whale Museum in Iceland, or a fishing village museum on the Japanese coast. Chosen from the map. Proves the thesis: the highest quality film at the lowest cost museum on Earth. This is the one the press writes about.

    What we learn before we claim anything

    Before saying "any museum, anywhere": ten small museums, ten mid sized, across at least six countries, each answering the intake questions below. That is the research phase and it runs alongside the pilots.

    Room07

    What we ask a museum first

    Ten questions that size the job and the budget before any work starts. An agent can pre fill most of them from the museum's website, annual report and social feeds; the call confirms the rest.

    1. Which story in the building deserves a film, and who inside cares most?
    2. Show us your most recent film. When was it made, what did it cost, how many staff hours went in?
    3. Who stopped the last update, and why? Money, time, rights, or nobody owned it?
    4. Who signs a contract of this size, and who would fund it: operating budget, a donor, a foundation, a tourism board?
    5. Which languages do your visitors actually speak? What do you offer them today?
    6. What assets exist: photographs, footage, objects, audio, research, a docent whose voice defines the place?
    7. Can the museum license its own imagery, speakers and music into an AI production? Who owns each piece?
    8. Who writes your AI policy, and does one exist? If not, would you adopt ours?
    9. Which of the nine accessibility items above do you need by law, and by when?
    10. What is the deadline: an opening, a season, a board meeting, a grant report?

    The intake algorithm

    From the answers, score four things: how long since the last film, how much it cost, how bad it is now, and how many languages are missing. High score, real deadline, one curator, usable assets: that museum gets a proposal the same week.

    Outreach and pitches

    The universal first email

    Subject: A film about [the object or story], for [museum]
    Hello [name], I make documentary films, and I have been building something for museums like yours: a way to turn the photographs, objects and research you already have into a short film worth watching, in your visitors' languages, for a fraction of what a production used to cost. I noticed [one specific thing: the exhibition opening in March, the 2004 theater film, the Japanese tour groups with no Japanese audio]. That is exactly the kind of story this is for. I am making three pilot films this autumn at no cost, in exchange for honest feedback. Would you be open to a twenty minute call to see if [museum] should be one of them? Mark Johnston Museum Mouse

    To a respected museum

    Your collection is the film. We do not bring a crew, a script from outside, or a style from somewhere else. We start with what your curators know and what your archive holds, and we build a documentary that meets the standard of the best museum films made anywhere, in every language your visitors speak, with accessibility built in rather than bolted on. A curator approves every fact before anything is animated. Generated imagery is disclosed and never presented as a record. You own the result outright. The difference is cost and time: weeks, not a year, and a price that fits inside one donor's gift.

    To a funder

    There are about 104,000 museums on Earth and almost none of them have a film, because a film used to require a crew and a year. That is no longer true, but the museums do not know it and could not run the tools if they did. Museum Mouse is the producer in the middle: documentary judgment on top of the best AI production tools, delivering broadcast quality films to institutions that have never had one. Your gift funds films for museums that cannot pay, starting with the smallest and most remote, and every film we make goes back into the world's open map of museums. It is a story about access, and it photographs well.

    To a producer who does not know AI

    You know what a five minute doc costs: a director, a DP, a crew day or two, an editor, a colorist, a composer, a mix, and eight weeks. Now imagine the archive is already shot, the transcripts write themselves, the first cut assembles overnight, the narration reads in twelve languages by lunch, and your job is the part you were always paid for: which story, which take, which cut, is it true. That is the whole company. The tools are cheap and getting better every month. Taste is the scarce thing, and museums have 104,000 stories waiting for it.

    Five years out

    Year one: three pilots, then paid films in the Rockies, Sweden and one remote region. Year two: a producer team of five, a partnership with one distribution platform, films back into Wikidata and OpenStreetMap. Year three: the atlas is the most complete map of museums in existence, the intake agents are finding and qualifying museums faster than the team can shoot, and the refresh business pays the rent. Year five: Museum Mouse is the default way a museum gets a film, in any language, at any size; the same engine tells city stories and small business stories; and the biggest museums in the world use it because it is as good, faster, and cheaper.
